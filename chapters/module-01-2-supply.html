Understanding Supply

The Law of Supply

Law of Supply
Holding all else equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises; when the price falls, the quantity supplied falls. The supply curve slopes upward from left to right.

Supply describes the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity that producers are willing and able to offer for sale. Unlike the inverse relationship of demand, supply slopes upward: higher prices create stronger incentives for producers to increase output. When oil prices rise, energy companies expand drilling operations, invest in new refineries, and increase production capacity—all motivated by the expectation of higher profits. When grain prices increase, farmers allocate more land to grain cultivation rather than other crops.

Just as with demand, we can represent supply with a supply schedule (a table) or a supply curve (a graph). The upward slope reflects the law of supply: producers supply more at higher prices. This reflects economic reality: firms have costs of production, and higher prices allow them to cover those costs and earn profits even when expanding production.

Supply Equations and Interpretation

Linear supply relationships typically take the form:

$$Q_s = c + dP$$

In this equation:

  • \(Q_s\) is the quantity supplied
  • P is the price of the good
  • c is the baseline quantity—the quantity supplied when price is zero. This is often zero or negative; a negative value represents a reservation price below which producers are unwilling to supply
  • d is the slope coefficient, a positive number. It tells us how much quantity supplied increases for each unit increase in price. A larger d means producers are more responsive to price changes

The positive coefficient on P is essential: it embodies the law of supply. If d = 15, it means that for every one-unit increase in price, quantity supplied increases by 15 units.

Building a Supply Schedule: The EU Sugar Market

Continuing with our EU sugar example, suppose economists have estimated the supply equation:

$$Q_s = 15P$$

Where \(Q_s\) is measured in millions of kilograms per year, and P is the price in euros per kilogram. Notice that the constant term (c) is zero, meaning that at a price of zero, producers supply nothing—which makes economic sense.

215(2) = 30

Notice the pattern: for each one-euro increase in price, quantity supplied increases by 15 million kilograms. The supply curve is perfectly linear, reflecting the simplifying assumption that the responsiveness to price is constant across all prices.

If the supply equation is \(Q_s = 15P\), how many millions of kilograms are supplied when the price is €3 per kilogram?
Substitute P = 3 into the equation: \(Q_s = 15(3) = 45\) million kilograms. At a price of €3 per kilogram, producers supply 45 million kilograms per year.

From Equation to Graph: Inverting the Supply Function

To graph the supply curve, we invert the supply equation so that price is a function of quantity:

Start with:
$$Q_s = 15P$$
Divide both sides by 15:
$$P = \frac{Q_s}{15}$$

The inverted form is \(P = Q_s/15\). Key features:

  • Vertical intercept: When \(Q_s = 0\), P = 0. The supply curve passes through the origin.
  • Slope: The coefficient on \(Q_s\) is 1/15. For every 15 units increase in quantity, price increases by 1 euro.

Graphing the Supply Curve

Method 1
Using the Origin and Slope: The curve passes through (0, 0). Slope is 1/15: move right 15 units and up 1 unit. Plot (15, 1), (30, 2), (45, 3), (60, 4), (75, 5). Connect with a straight line.
Method 2
Plotting Schedule Points: Use the supply schedule. Plot (15, 1), (30, 2), (45, 3), (60, 4), (75, 5). Connect with a straight line.

The supply curve slopes upward, confirming the law of supply. Notice that it is less steep than the demand curve (which has a slope of -1/10 in absolute value). The supply curve has a slope of 1/15 ≈ 0.067. This means demand is more responsive to price changes than supply in this particular market.

Important Distinction: Supply vs. Quantity Supplied

"Supply" refers to the entire curve—the full relationship between price and quantity supplied. "Quantity supplied" refers to a specific point on that curve at a particular price. When the supply curve shifts (which we will explore in Module 2), economists say "supply increased" or "supply decreased." Movements along the supply curve due to price changes are changes in "quantity supplied," not changes in "supply."

Using the supply equation \(Q_s = 15P\), how much does quantity supplied increase if price rises from €2 to €4 per kilogram?
At P = 2: \(Q_s = 15(2) = 30\) million kg. At P = 4: \(Q_s = 15(4) = 60\) million kg. Quantity supplied increases by 30 million kilograms — a doubling of output in response to a doubling of price.
