Understanding Supply

The Law of Supply

Law of Supply
Holding all else equal (ceteris paribus), when the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises; when the price falls, the quantity supplied falls. The supply curve slopes upward from left to right.

Supply describes the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity that producers are willing and able to offer for sale. Unlike the inverse relationship of demand, supply slopes upward: higher prices create stronger incentives for producers to increase output. When the price of a good rises, producers find it profitable to expand output—by hiring more workers, running facilities longer, or diverting resources from lower-value uses. When price falls, those incentives weaken and producers scale back.

Just as with demand, we can represent supply with a supply schedule (a table) or a supply curve (a graph). The upward slope reflects the law of supply: producers supply more at higher prices. This reflects economic reality: firms have costs of production, and higher prices allow them to cover those costs and earn profits even when expanding production.

In the CAP setting, this is the other half of the story: if the policy guarantees a higher sugar price, farmers respond by producing more. Part 3 will show why combining that upward-sloping supply curve with the downward-sloping demand curve creates surplus.

Supply Equations and Interpretation

Linear supply relationships typically take the form:

$$Q_s = c + dP$$

In this equation:

  • \(Q_s\) is the quantity supplied
  • P is the price of the good
  • c is the baseline quantity — the quantity supplied when price is zero. This is often zero or negative; a negative value represents a reservation price below which producers are unwilling to supply
  • d is the slope coefficient, a positive number. It tells us how much quantity supplied increases for each one-unit increase in price. A larger d means producers are more responsive to price changes

The positive coefficient on P is essential: it embodies the law of supply.

From Equation to Graph: Inverting the Supply Function

To graph the supply curve with price on the vertical axis, we invert the supply equation so that P is a function of quantity:

Start with:
$$Q_s = 15P$$
Divide both sides by 15:
$$P = \frac{Q_s}{15}$$

The inverted form is \(P = Q_s/15\). Key features:

  • Vertical intercept: When \(Q_s = 0\), P = 0. The supply curve passes through the origin.
  • Slope: The coefficient on \(Q_s\) is 1/15. For every 15 million kg increase in quantity, price increases by €1.
What to Notice

Supply is simpler to invert here than demand because the equation has no constant term: \(Q_s = 15P\) becomes \(P = Q_s/15\). That immediately tells you two things students should internalize: the curve passes through the origin, and its slope is positive.

  • Slope: The coefficient on \(Q_s\) is 1/3. For every 3 million additional prescriptions supplied, the co-pay rises by $1.

Graphing the Supply Curve

Important Distinction: Supply vs. Quantity Supplied

"Supply" refers to the entire curve — the full relationship between price and quantity supplied. "Quantity supplied" refers to a specific point on that curve at a particular price. When the supply curve shifts (which we will explore in Module 2), economists say "supply increased" or "supply decreased." Movements along the supply curve due to price changes are changes in "quantity supplied," not changes in "supply."

Common Mistake

Two errors show up repeatedly here. First, students sometimes draw supply downward because they are still thinking about demand. Second, they say "supply increased" when price rises. In Module 1, a price rise means a movement along the supply curve, so only quantity supplied increases.

Supply in Your Policy Context

In the EU sugar market, the supply equation \(Q_s = 15P\) reflects the behavior of European sugar beet farmers: higher prices induce more production. The supply curve passes through the origin, meaning no sugar is produced at a price of zero — consistent with economic logic. This simple linear supply function will underpin our analysis of the CAP price support in Part 3.

In the generic atorvastatin market, the supply side changed dramatically after patent expiry in November 2011. Before expiry: one manufacturer (Pfizer), constrained production, high price. After expiry: dozens of FDA-approved generic firms entered, each able to produce at large scale and low average cost. In our model, \(Q_s = 3P\) with d = 3 captures the aggregate responsiveness of all generic manufacturers: higher prices attract more production. In Part 3 we will overlay this supply curve with the demand curve and find the price and quantity at which the two sides of the market are in balance — and see precisely how much prices fell and quantities rose after patent expiry, exactly as the Hatch-Waxman Act intended.

In the retail gasoline market, supply is shaped by the refining and distribution sector. In the short run, refineries respond slowly to price changes — they take time to expand capacity, and pipelines and tankers have fixed throughput. But over longer horizons, higher prices do attract more production. In our model, \(Q_s = 20P\) captures this price responsiveness in a simplified way. The 1973 OPEC oil embargo effectively reduced the amount of crude reaching US refineries — producers could supply far less gasoline at every price level than before. Part 3 will show what happens to price and quantity when the amount producers can supply at every price suddenly falls.

In the Colorado legal cannabis market, supply comes from licensed cultivators and dispensaries regulated by the state. Higher prices attract more licensed producers and larger harvests, consistent with the law of supply. In our model, \(Q_s = 2P\) captures this relationship. Colorado's cannabis taxes include a 15% wholesale excise tax and a special retail sales tax (10% when legal sales began in January 2014 under Amendment 64, raised to 15% in 2017); the "15% plus 15%" figures sometimes cited reflect the post-2017 rates. Together these taxes create a wedge between the price consumers pay and the revenue producers keep. In Part 3 we will see how this tax drives a gap between the two prices, and how the size of d (price responsiveness) determines how the tax burden is split between consumers and producers.

In the EU sugar supply equation \(Q_s = 15P\), the slope coefficient d = 15. What does this number tell you about sugar beet farmers as a group — and how would a larger value of d change your interpretation of their behavior?
d = 15 means that for every €1 increase in price, farmers collectively supply 15 million more kilograms per year. A larger d would mean producers are more responsive to price signals: the same price increase triggers a much larger increase in output. This could reflect things like easily expandable farmland, low costs of switching between crops, or a large number of farms that can pivot quickly. A smaller d means the same price increase yields little extra output — perhaps because production capacity is fixed in the short run.
The generic atorvastatin supply equation has d = 3. What does this coefficient represent, and why might d be much larger several years after patent expiry than in the months immediately following it?
d = 3 means each $1 increase in the monthly co-pay brings 3 million additional prescriptions to market. It captures the collective price-responsiveness of all generic manufacturers. In the first months after patent expiry, few generics have FDA approval, so d is small — the market responds weakly to higher prices. Several years later, many manufacturers are approved, production lines are running, and the same price increase can trigger a much larger supply response. d therefore rises over time as the generic industry scales up, which is why generic prices typically fall steeply in the years following patent expiry.
The gasoline supply equation uses d = 20, but a footnote notes that actual short-run supply responds much less strongly to price. What does this gap between model and reality tell you about the limitations of a single slope coefficient? When is a high-d supply equation a reasonable simplification, and when is it misleading?
A high d means producers respond strongly to price changes: each extra dollar per gallon calls forth 20 billion more gallons. In reality, refineries cannot quickly expand throughput — pipelines, storage tanks, and refining capacity are all fixed in the short run. So d overstates actual short-run responsiveness. A high-d equation is a reasonable simplification for long-run analysis (where capacity can be built) or for illustrating how supply curves work without making the algebra complex. It becomes misleading when used to predict how quickly supply would respond to a sudden price spike — in the short run, the supply curve is much steeper than our model implies.
In the Colorado legal cannabis supply equation \(Q_s = 2P\), d = 2. Colorado's licensing regime limits who can legally grow and sell. How does that regulatory constraint show up in the value of d — and what would you expect to happen to d if the state significantly expanded the number of licenses?
d = 2 reflects the price-responsiveness of all licensed producers combined. Because licensing is restricted, the number of firms that can respond to higher prices is capped: when prices rise, existing licensees can expand somewhat, but new entrants cannot join freely. This keeps d relatively low. If the state issued many more licenses, more producers could enter the market in response to price signals, and the same $1 increase in price would call forth more than 2 million additional grams — d would rise. Regulatory constraints are therefore one real-world factor that directly shapes the slope of a supply curve.
