Introduction

Welcome to the first of three modules designed to prepare you for API-101 at the Harvard Kennedy School. This module introduces the core building blocks of microeconomic analysis: demand, supply, and equilibrium. These concepts form the analytical foundation for virtually every policy question you will encounter in the course.

Economics is fundamentally a framework for understanding choices and their consequences. When a government implements a policy—whether it is setting a minimum wage, imposing a tariff, or subsidizing an industry—the policy operates through supply and demand mechanisms. To predict and evaluate the effects of policy, you must understand how buyers and sellers respond to incentives, and how prices coordinate their actions.

Each section combines reading material, interactive demonstrations, and self-check opportunities. We will use the European sugar market as our primary worked example throughout, building your understanding step by step. By the end of this module, you will be able to graph demand and supply curves, solve for equilibrium algebraically, interpret what happens when markets are out of balance, and connect these concepts to real policy choices.

Policy Connections

What's Under the CAP?

Economics provides a framework for thinking about the impact of decisions and market forces on complex policy scenarios. Throughout these modules, we will use the European Union's Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) as a running example to connect foundational economics to real-world policy. The CAP offers an excellent case study because it illustrates how government intervention, even when well-intentioned, can create unintended economic consequences that supply-and-demand analysis helps us understand.

The EU Common Agricultural Policy: Origins and Structure

The Common Agricultural Policy, established in 1962, is one of the oldest and most significant policy interventions in modern markets. The EU created the CAP to address what policymakers saw as critical concerns: ensuring a stable food supply for Europe's consumers and guaranteeing decent living standards for the continent's farmers. In the decades after World War II, food security was a genuine policy priority, and agriculture represented a much larger share of employment and output than it does today.

The original CAP operated through a system of price supports: the government would set a guaranteed minimum price for various agricultural products. If the market price fell below this guaranteed price, the government would step in and purchase the unsold surplus at the guaranteed price, storing it or sometimes disposing of it. This policy succeeded in boosting farm incomes and agricultural output—perhaps too well. Over time, the guaranteed prices created powerful incentives for farmers to expand production far beyond what the market demanded at any reasonable price. The result was massive, persistent surpluses.

By the 1980s and 1990s, the oversupply had reached absurd proportions. Europe accumulated what the press nicknamed "Butter Mountains"—enormous stockpiles of stored butter—and "Wine Lakes"—vast quantities of unsellable wine. Beginning in the 1990s, the CAP underwent significant reforms, shifting toward direct payments to farmers that are partially decoupled from production. However, the original CAP structure with price supports provides an excellent lens for understanding how markets work—which is exactly what we will develop in this module.

Why Start with the CAP?

When you understand demand and supply, you can predict why price supports above equilibrium create surpluses. By the end of Part 3, you will be able to calculate exactly how large those surpluses were, at what fiscal cost to EU taxpayers, and why the policy had to change. These tools are the foundation for the rest of the preparation sequence and for policy analysis more broadly.

When the Price of a Drug Falls 85% Overnight

When Pfizer's patent on Lipitor expired in November 2011, something remarkable happened: the price of atorvastatin—a cholesterol-lowering medication taken by millions of Americans—fell by roughly 85% within months. Pharmacies that had rarely stocked it suddenly kept large supplies. Doctors wrote more prescriptions. Patients who had previously skipped doses or gone without could now afford the drug.

What Changed When the Patent Expired?

For more than a decade, Pfizer was the only company legally allowed to sell atorvastatin in the United States. When the patent expired, dozens of other manufacturers received FDA approval to produce and sell the same drug. Suddenly, instead of one seller, buyers had many sellers competing for their business. All that competition created powerful pressure for prices to fall—and fall they did.

The Drug Price Competition and Patent Term Restoration Act of 1984 (the Hatch-Waxman Act) created the legal process that makes this happen for every major drug whose patent expires. It is one of the most consequential pieces of health policy legislation in American history—and its effects play out in a way that supply-and-demand analysis describes precisely. Throughout this module, we will develop the tools to understand exactly why competition drives prices down and what determines how far.

Why the Generic Drug Market?

The atorvastatin example illustrates a pattern that repeats across the pharmaceutical industry: patents expire, competitors enter, prices fall, more patients are treated. By the end of Part 3, you will be able to use our model to analyze how a government price ceiling on prescription drugs would create a shortage—and why that outcome is surprisingly similar to what happened with gasoline in the 1970s.

Lines at the Gas Station: The 1970s Energy Crisis

In the fall of 1973, Arab oil-producing nations declared an embargo against the United States, sharply cutting the flow of crude oil to American refiners. Gasoline became scarce. Drivers waited in lines for hours—sometimes wrapping around the block—to fill their tanks. Some stations ran dry and closed. State governments resorted to odd-even rationing: drivers with odd-numbered license plates could only buy on odd-numbered days, and even-numbered plates on even days.

Why Did Price Controls Make It Worse?

What made the shortage so severe was not just the oil cutoff—it was that President Nixon had already imposed economy-wide price controls in August 1971, which covered gasoline and petroleum products. Those controls kept the official price of gasoline artificially low even as the underlying oil became scarce. The result: buyers wanted far more gasoline than sellers could profitably supply at the controlled price. The gap showed up not as a higher price, but as empty stations and long lines.

When price controls were finally lifted, in stages through the late 1970s and early 1980s, prices rose—but the lines disappeared. Markets can handle scarcity, but only if price signals are allowed to function. This is one of the clearest lessons from twentieth-century economic policy, and it is exactly what supply-and-demand analysis predicts. Throughout this module, we will develop the tools to understand why prices matter and what happens when they are prevented from adjusting.

Why the Gasoline Market?

The 1970s energy crisis is perhaps the most vivid real-world demonstration of what happens when policy holds prices below where buyers and sellers would naturally agree. By the end of Part 3, you will be able to calculate exactly how large a shortage the price controls created, and why lifting them—though painful—restored balance to the market.

From Prohibition to Dispensaries: Colorado's Cannabis Market

When Colorado voters passed Amendment 64 in November 2012, they did something unusual: they created a brand-new legal market where no legal market had existed before. Before the amendment, all cannabis transactions in Colorado were illegal. Afterward, licensed cultivators and dispensaries could grow and sell cannabis openly—subject to health regulations, licensing requirements, and taxes.

What Happened After Legalization?

Prices of legal cannabis fell substantially over the years following legalization as more sellers entered the market and production techniques improved. At the same time, Colorado imposed significant taxes—combining excise and sales taxes to roughly 30% of the purchase price. This tax makes legal cannabis more expensive for consumers than it would otherwise be. Higher prices mean some buyers purchase less, or turn to informal channels instead.

State policymakers face a real tradeoff: higher taxes generate more revenue per unit sold, but they also raise the price consumers pay, which reduces how much they buy through legal channels. The same tradeoff appears in tobacco, alcohol, and carbon policy. Throughout this module, we will develop the tools to understand this tradeoff precisely—how much does a tax raise the price consumers pay, how much does it reduce the quantity sold, and who ultimately bears the cost?

Why the Cannabis Market?

Colorado's cannabis market is a rare natural experiment: a brand-new competitive market created by policy. By the end of Part 3, you will be able to use our model to analyze how Colorado's excise tax affects the price consumers pay, the quantity sold, and what share of the tax burden falls on buyers versus sellers—insights that generalize directly to tobacco, alcohol, and carbon taxes.

Understanding Demand

The Law of Demand

Law of Demand
Holding all else equal — ceteris paribus, a Latin phrase economists use to mean "all other things held constant" — when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls; when the price falls, the quantity demanded rises. The relationship is inverse, and the demand curve slopes downward from left to right.

Economists use the term "demand" to describe the relationship between price and the quantity that consumers are willing and able to purchase. This relationship is based on needs, wants, and the ability to pay. When the price of a product increases, consumers typically respond by purchasing less of it—a phenomenon we see everywhere, from gasoline prices to concert tickets to smartphone models. When the price falls, they purchase more.

The law of demand reflects rational economic behavior: consumers face trade-offs. When sugar becomes more expensive, households might consume less directly, switch to artificial sweeteners, or purchase fewer sugary products overall. Bakeries and beverage manufacturers might reduce their use of sugar or develop alternative recipes. Some potential customers may be priced out of the market entirely. Conversely, when sugar becomes cheaper, all these decisions reverse—consumers purchase more.

That CAP connection matters immediately: if policy keeps the sugar price above the market-clearing level, this demand relationship tells us that buyers will purchase less sugar, not more.

The law of demand reflects rational economic behavior: consumers face trade-offs. When a prescription drug becomes more expensive, some patients may skip doses, split pills, or ask their doctor for a cheaper alternative. Insurance plans may remove it from their preferred formulary. Some lower-income patients may be priced out of the drug entirely. Conversely, when the price falls—as it did dramatically after generic atorvastatin entered the market—more patients fill their prescriptions, and more completely.

The law of demand reflects rational economic behavior: consumers face trade-offs. When gasoline becomes more expensive, drivers may combine errands, carpool more, switch to public transit, or eventually trade in a gas-guzzler for a more fuel-efficient vehicle. Businesses reduce delivery frequency or pass higher costs on to consumers. Some discretionary trips simply don't happen. Conversely, when gasoline becomes cheaper, all these adjustments reverse—people drive more.

The law of demand reflects rational economic behavior: consumers face trade-offs. When legal cannabis becomes more expensive—because of a tax increase, for example—some consumers buy less, others switch to informal channels, and some reduce their use altogether. Conversely, when the price of legal cannabis falls, more consumers find it worthwhile to buy from licensed dispensaries. The law of demand captures this inverse relationship between price and quantity purchased.

A demand schedule is a table showing the quantity demanded at various price levels. When you plot these price-quantity pairs on a graph, you create a demand curve. By convention in economics, we place quantity on the horizontal axis (x-axis) and price on the vertical axis (y-axis). This convention—which is opposite to typical mathematical practice where the independent variable goes on the x-axis—has historical roots and is universal in economics. The resulting downward-sloping line illustrates the law of demand visually.

Demand Equations: Reading the Math

Linear demand relationships can be expressed algebraically in standard form as:

$$Q_d = a - bP$$

In this equation:

  • \(Q_d\) is the quantity demanded (the dependent variable in mathematical terms, though we place it on the horizontal axis when graphing)
  • P is the price of the good
  • a is the quantity intercept — the quantity demanded when price equals zero. On a graph with quantity on the horizontal axis, this is where the demand curve meets the horizontal axis. It represents the maximum quantity consumers would buy if the good were free
  • b is the slope coefficient, a positive number. It tells us how much quantity demanded decreases for each one-unit increase in price. The negative sign in front of bP ensures the relationship is inverse — higher price, lower quantity demanded

The negative coefficient on P is crucial: it embodies the law of demand. If b = 10, it means that for every one-unit increase in price, quantity demanded falls by 10 units.

From Equation to Graph: Inverting the Demand Function

To graph the demand curve, we need to invert the demand equation so that price (P) is expressed as a function of quantity (\(Q_d\)). This is necessary because we place price on the vertical axis. Let's work through the algebra step by step.

Start with:
$$Q_d = 50 - 10P$$
Add \(10P\) to both sides:
$$Q_d + 10P = 50$$
Subtract \(Q_d\) from both sides:
$$10P = 50 - Q_d$$
Divide both sides by 10:
$$P = 5 - \frac{Q_d}{10}$$

The inverted form is \(P = 5 - Q_d/10\). Two key features:

  • Vertical intercept: When \(Q_d = 0\), P = 5. The demand curve crosses the price axis at €5/kg — the highest price any consumer will pay.
  • Slope: The coefficient on \(Q_d\) is −1/10. For every 10-million-kg increase in quantity, price falls by €1.
What to Notice

Inverting the equation does not change the economics. It is the same demand relationship, rewritten so it fits the standard economics graph with quantity on the horizontal axis and price on the vertical axis. This is why the transcript spends time on inversion: it is the bridge between algebra and the graph.

Notice that Methods 1, 2, and 3 all produce exactly the same demand curve — because there is only one straight line consistent with the equation. The intercept method is fastest when you have the inverted equation. The slope-and-a-point method is useful when you know one coordinate and the slope. Important Distinction: Demand vs. Quantity Demanded

"Demand" refers to the entire relationship between price and quantity — the entire demand curve. "Quantity demanded" refers to a specific amount at a specific price: one point on that curve. When economists say "demand increased," they mean the entire curve shifted. When price changes and consumers respond by buying more or less, quantity demanded changes but demand (the curve itself) does not. This distinction becomes crucial in Module 2.

Common Mistake

Students often mix up the intercepts because the graph uses economics conventions, not standard algebra conventions. For demand, the price intercept belongs on the vertical axis and the quantity intercept belongs on the horizontal axis. A second common mistake is to say "demand changed" when only price changed; in that case, only quantity demanded changes.
