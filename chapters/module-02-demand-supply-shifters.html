Demand and Supply Shifters

Beyond price: understanding the forces that move entire demand and supply curves and reshape market equilibria.

Learning Objectives
  • Identify the key factors that shift the demand curve
  • Identify the key factors that shift the supply curve
  • Distinguish between normal goods and inferior goods
  • Explain how substitute and complementary goods affect demand
  • Apply the four-step process to analyze equilibrium changes
  • Predict price and quantity changes when curves shift

Introduction

In Module 1, you learned how the law of demand and law of supply create the downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve. Both relationships hold true under a critical assumption: ceteris paribus, or "all else equal." But in the real world, "all else" is constantly changing. Consumer tastes shift, production technology advances, input costs rise and fall, and new competitors enter or leave markets.

This module extends your understanding of markets by examining what happens when those other factors change. You will learn which factors cause demand curves to shift, which cause supply curves to shift, and how these shifts propagate through markets to create new equilibria. This is the heart of economic policy analysis: understanding how interventions, shocks, or natural events reshape market outcomes.

Critical Distinction

Movement along a curve: A change in the price of the good itself causes a movement along the demand or supply curve. The curve itself does not move.

Shift of the curve: A change in any other factor (income, tastes, input costs, technology, etc.) shifts the entire curve to the left or right. This is a fundamentally different effect on the market.

Factors That Shift Demand

A demand curve represents the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price, holding everything else constant. When any of those other factors change, the entire curve shifts—meaning at every price level, a different quantity is demanded. Understanding which factors shift demand is essential for predicting how markets respond to real-world changes.

Demand Shifters
Factors that cause the entire demand curve to shift to a new position:
  • Consumer Income: How much money households have to spend
  • Tastes and Preferences: Consumer attitudes toward the good
  • Prices of Substitutes: Prices of goods that can replace this good
  • Prices of Complements: Prices of goods used together with this good
  • Expectations: Beliefs about future prices or income
  • Number of Buyers: Size and composition of the market

Normal Goods vs. Inferior Goods

Not all goods respond to income changes in the same way. When consumer income rises, demand for most goods increases—these are called normal goods. Examples include restaurant meals, new cars, and vacation travel. The demand curve shifts to the right: at any price, consumers want to buy more.

For a few goods, the opposite happens. When income rises, demand decreases—these are inferior goods. Classic examples are generic-brand products, used cars, or inexpensive housing. As people become wealthier, they buy less of these goods and switch to higher-quality alternatives. The demand curve shifts to the left.

Normal Good
A good whose demand increases when consumer income rises and decreases when income falls.
Inferior Good
A good whose demand decreases when consumer income rises and increases when income falls. This label describes a relationship between the good and income, not a judgment about the good's quality.

Substitutes and Complements

The prices of other goods in the market strongly influence demand for any particular good. Goods can be related in two key ways:

Substitute Goods
Goods that consumers can use in place of each other. When the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original good increases (consumers switch to it). When the price of a substitute falls, demand for the original good decreases (consumers switch away from it).
Complementary Goods
Goods that are typically used together. When the price of a complement rises, demand for the original good decreases (fewer people buy the pair). When the price of a complement falls, demand for the original good increases (more people buy the pair).
Example: Substitutes and Complements

Substitutes: When the price of butter rises sharply, many consumers switch to margarine. Demand for margarine increases—the demand curve for margarine shifts right.

Complements: Gasoline and sport utility vehicles (SUVs) are complements. When gasoline prices skyrocket, demand for SUVs decreases because driving becomes more expensive. The demand curve for SUVs shifts left. Conversely, if gasoline prices fall, demand for SUVs rises.

Self-Check: A major medical study announces that coffee consumption is linked to serious health risks. What happens to the demand curve for coffee?
The demand curve for coffee shifts to the left (decreases). This is a change in consumer tastes and preferences—a key demand shifter. At any given price, fewer people are now willing to buy coffee. This would be distinguished from a movement along the demand curve, which would occur if coffee's own price changed.

Factors That Shift Supply

Just as demand curves shift when underlying conditions change, so do supply curves. A supply curve shows the quantity firms are willing to produce and sell at each price. When the cost or feasibility of production changes, the entire curve shifts. Producers will offer more of a good if it becomes cheaper or easier to make, and less if it becomes more expensive or difficult.

Supply Shifters
Factors that cause the entire supply curve to shift to a new position:
  • Input Prices: Costs of labor, materials, energy, and other production factors
  • Technology: Methods and efficiency of production
  • Natural Conditions: Weather, natural disasters, or resource availability
  • Government Regulations and Taxes: Rules and costs imposed on producers
  • Producer Expectations: Beliefs about future prices or conditions
  • Number of Sellers: How many firms are in the market

How Changes in Production Costs Affect Supply

Firms make production decisions based on profit—the difference between revenue and costs. When production costs fall, firms can produce the same quantity at lower cost, making previously unprofitable production economically viable. Supply increases; the curve shifts right. When production costs rise, supply decreases; the curve shifts left.

Example: If steel prices (a key input for cars) fall, automakers can produce cars profitably at lower prices. The supply of cars increases—the supply curve shifts right. At every price level, automakers are willing to produce more cars. Conversely, if steel prices spike, supply decreases and the curve shifts left.

Technology and Productivity

When firms discover new production methods or technologies that reduce costs, supply increases dramatically. A classic example is the Green Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s, when improved seeds for wheat and rice doubled crop yields in low-income countries. Supply curves for these crops shifted right, and more food became available at every price level.

Self-Check: A breakthrough in drilling technology makes extracting oil significantly cheaper. What happens to the supply curve for oil?
The supply curve for oil shifts to the right (increases). Lower production costs mean firms can produce oil profitably at lower prices. At any given price, more oil is supplied. This is a positive supply shock that increases market supply and typically lowers the equilibrium price.
Important Principle

A shift in one curve never causes a shift in the other curve. Instead, a shift in one curve causes a movement along the other curve. For example, if supply decreases (curve shifts left), the equilibrium price rises, causing a movement up and to the left along the demand curve. But the demand curve itself does not shift.

The Four-Step Process for Equilibrium Analysis

When an economic event affects demand or supply, how do you predict the new equilibrium price and quantity? Use this systematic four-step process. It works for any market and any type of change.

1
Draw the Initial Equilibrium

Sketch the original demand and supply curves and mark the equilibrium point where they intersect. Label the original equilibrium price (P*) and quantity (Q*). This is your baseline for comparison.

2
Identify What Changed

Read the scenario carefully. Is the change an event that affects demand (tastes, income, substitute prices, etc.) or supply (input costs, technology, regulations, etc.)? Or does it affect both? Be precise about which factor has changed.

3
Determine the Direction of Shift

Does the change cause the affected curve to shift right (increase) or left (decrease)? Use the definitions and logic of demand and supply shifters. For example: higher input costs → supply decreases → curve shifts left. Lower interest rates → consumers can borrow more → demand increases → curve shifts right.

4
Find and Compare the New Equilibrium

Mark the new intersection point of the curves. Compare the new equilibrium price and quantity to the original. Did price rise or fall? Did quantity rise or fall? State your conclusion clearly. If both curves shifted, you may be certain about one variable but not the other.

Worked Example: Good Weather for Salmon

In summer 2000, California's salmon fishing industry experienced unusually favorable conditions: heavy rains increased river water levels, cooler ocean temperatures boosted plankton growth, and calm seas meant fewer lost fishing days. How did these conditions affect equilibrium salmon price and quantity?

Applying the Four-Step Process

Step 1: Original Equilibrium
Before the good weather, demand and supply intersected at an equilibrium price of $3.25 per pound and equilibrium quantity of 250,000 fish.

Step 2: What Changed?
Good weather is a natural condition that affects production. This is a supply shifter, not a demand shifter.

Step 3: Direction of Shift
Good weather increases fish stocks and makes fishing more productive. The supply curve shifts to the right. More fish are available at every price level.

Step 4: New Equilibrium
With supply increased, the supply curve moves right and intersects demand at a new point. The new equilibrium has:
— Quantity: 550,000 fish (increased from 250,000)
— Price: $2.50 per pound (decreased from $3.25)

Conclusion: Good weather increased supply, which increased the equilibrium quantity of salmon but decreased the price. Consumers benefited from lower prices and more availability; fishers benefited from higher catches but faced lower prices per unit.

Policy Connections: The EU Common Agricultural Policy

The European Union's Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) is one of the world's largest agricultural subsidy programs, distributing billions of euros annually to farmers. Understanding the CAP requires understanding supply shifters.

How the CAP Works as a Supply Shifter

The CAP directly subsidizes agricultural production—farmers receive payments based on output or land use. This effectively lowers producers' net costs. A subsidy shifts the supply curve to the right: farmers are willing to supply more output at every price, or the same output at lower prices.

The CAP also includes price supports that guarantee farmers a minimum price. This prevents prices from falling too far, even when supply is abundant. The result: larger quantities of agricultural products at higher prices than would occur in an unsubsidized market.

The trade-off: While CAP protects farmers' incomes and supports rural communities, it also increases food prices for European consumers and distorts global agricultural markets—foreign farmers cannot compete with subsidized EU production. This illustrates how understanding supply shifters (in this case, subsidies as a shifter) is essential for evaluating policy consequences.

Key Terms

Ceteris Paribus

The assumption that all relevant factors except those being studied are held constant; Latin for "all else equal."

Demand Shifter

Any factor other than price that affects quantity demanded; includes income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations, and number of buyers.

Supply Shifter

Any factor other than price that affects quantity supplied; includes input prices, technology, natural conditions, regulations, expectations, and number of sellers.

Normal Good

A good whose demand increases when consumer income rises and decreases when income falls.

Inferior Good

A good whose demand decreases when consumer income rises and increases when income falls.

Substitutes

Goods that can be used in place of each other; a higher price for one increases demand for the other.

Complements

Goods typically used together; a higher price for one decreases demand for the other.

Movement Along a Curve

A change in quantity demanded or supplied caused by a change in the good's own price; the curve itself does not move.

Shift of a Curve

A change in demand or supply caused by factors other than the good's own price; the entire curve moves to a new position.

Summary

Module 2 expanded your toolkit for understanding markets. You learned that price is not the only factor affecting markets; income, tastes, technology, input costs, and expectations all drive shifts in demand and supply. You mastered the critical distinction between movements along curves (caused by price changes) and shifts of curves (caused by other factors). Most importantly, you learned the four-step process for analyzing how any market change affects equilibrium price and quantity.

These skills are the foundation for applied economics and policy analysis. In Module 3, you will learn about consumer and producer surplus—the tools that economists use to evaluate whether policies make markets better or worse off.
