Module 4

Demand and Supply Shifters

Beyond price: understanding the forces that move entire demand and supply curves and reshape market equilibria.

Learning Objectives
  • Identify the key factors that shift the demand curve
  • Identify the key factors that shift the supply curve
  • Distinguish between normal goods and inferior goods
  • Explain how substitute and complementary goods affect demand
  • Model a demand or supply shift algebraically and graphically
  • Apply the four-step process to analyze equilibrium changes
  • Predict price and quantity changes when curves shift

Introduction

In Modules 1-3, you learned how the law of demand and law of supply create the downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve, then how those curves determine equilibrium. Both relationships hold true under a critical assumption: ceteris paribus, or "all else equal." But in the real world, "all else" is constantly changing. Consumer tastes shift, production technologies advance, input costs rise and fall, and new competitors enter or leave markets.

This module extends your understanding of markets by examining what happens when those other factors change. You will learn which factors cause demand curves to shift, which cause supply curves to shift, and how these shifts propagate through markets to create new equilibria. This is the heart of economic policy analysis: understanding how interventions, shocks, or natural events reshape market outcomes.

As in Modules 1-3, the EU's Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) serves as our running example. After analyzing implications of supply and demand shifts, we will be able to study the consequences of the CAP price support intervention on domestic and foreign markets.

Critical Distinction

Movement along a curve: A change in the price of the good itself causes a movement along the demand or supply curve. The curve itself does not move.

Shift of the curve: A change in any other factor (income, tastes, input costs, technology, etc.) shifts the entire curve to the left or right. This is a fundamentally different effect on the market.

Factors That Shift Demand

A demand curve represents the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price, holding everything else constant. When any of those other factors change, the entire curve shifts — meaning at every price level, a different quantity is demanded. Understanding which factors shift demand is essential for predicting how markets respond to real-world changes.

Demand Shifters
Factors that cause the entire demand curve to shift to a new position:
  • Consumer Income: How much money households have to spend
  • Tastes and Preferences: Consumer attitudes toward the good
  • Prices of Substitutes: Prices of goods that can replace this good
  • Prices of Complements: Prices of goods used together with this good
  • Expectations: Beliefs about future prices or income
  • Number of Buyers: Size and composition of the market

Normal Goods vs. Inferior Goods

Not all goods respond to income changes in the same way. When consumer income rises, demand for most goods increases — these are called normal goods. Examples include restaurant meals, new cars, and vacation travel. The demand curve shifts to the right: at any price, consumers want to buy more.

For a few goods, the opposite happens. When income rises, demand decreases — these are inferior goods. Classic examples are generic-brand products, used cars, or bus rides. As people become wealthier, they buy less of these goods and switch to higher-quality alternatives. The demand curve shifts to the left.

Normal Good
A good whose demand increases when consumer income rises and decreases when income falls.
Inferior Good
A good whose demand decreases when consumer income rises and increases when income falls. This label describes a relationship between the good and income, not a judgment about the good's quality.

Substitutes and Complements

The prices of other goods in the market strongly influence demand for any particular good. Goods can be related in two key ways:

Substitute Goods
Goods that consumers can use in place of each other. When the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original good increases (consumers switch to it). When the price of a substitute falls, demand for the original good decreases (consumers switch out of it).
Complementary Goods
Goods that are typically consumed together. When the price of a complement rises, demand for the original good decreases (fewer people buy both). When the price of a complement falls, demand for the original good increases (more people buy both).

Example: Substitutes and Complements

Related goods change demand by changing how attractive the original good feels at every price. If a substitute becomes more expensive, buyers switch to the original good and its demand shifts right. If a complement becomes more expensive, so does the original good, and its demand shifts left.

Factors That Shift Supply

Just as demand curves shift when underlying conditions change, so do supply curves. A supply curve shows the quantity firms are willing to produce and sell at each price. When the cost or feasibility of production changes, the entire curve shifts. Producers will offer more of a good if it becomes cheaper or easier to make, and less if it becomes more expensive or difficult.

Supply Shifters
Factors that cause the entire supply curve to shift to a new position:
  • Input Prices: Costs of labor, materials, energy, and other production factors
  • Technology: Methods and efficiency of production
  • Natural Conditions: Weather, natural disasters, or resource availability
  • Producer Expectations: Beliefs about future prices or conditions
  • Number of Sellers: How many firms are in the market

How Changes in Production Costs Affect Supply

Firms make production decisions based on profit — the difference between revenue and costs. When production costs fall, firms can produce the same quantity at lower cost, making previously unprofitable production economically viable. Supply increases and the supply curve shifts right. When production costs rise, supply decreases and the supply curve shifts left.

Technology and Productivity

When firms discover new production methods or technologies that reduce costs, supply increases dramatically. A classic example is the Green Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s, when improved seeds for wheat and rice doubled crop yields in low-income countries. Supply curves for these crops shifted right, and more food became available at every price level.

Illustrating Supply Shifts

Two common supply shifters move the supply curve in opposite directions. Bad weather makes production harder and shifts supply left. Better technology makes production easier and shifts supply right.

Figure 4.2: Drought or bad weather shifts supply left.

Figure 4.3: Better technology shifts supply right.

Exceptionally hot weather and very little rainfall reduce agricultural yields. What happens to the supply curve for sugar?
The supply curve for sugar shifts to the left (decreases). Poor weather makes production more difficult and reduces output at every price. This is a negative supply shock — different from a movement along the supply curve, which would occur only if sugar's own price changed.
Important Principle

A shift in one curve never causes a shift in the other curve. Instead, a shift in one curve causes a movement along the other curve. For example, if supply decreases (curve shifts left), the equilibrium price rises, causing a movement up and to the left along the demand curve. But the demand curve itself does not shift.

Policy Connection: CAP as a Market Shifter

The EU's Common Agricultural Policy illustrates demand and supply analysis in action. Supporting high prices through government purchases created large agricultural surpluses, which were sometimes dumped on foreign markets, particularly in developing countries. In this module, we examine the effect of CAP on the EU market as a demand shifter and then turn to its effect on the supply of agricultural products in developing-country markets to study the policy's impact on foreign producers.

Price Support: A Demand Shifter in the EU Market

CAP's price support mechanism guaranteed farmers a minimum price for their output. Unlike the simple price floor we explored in Module 3, price support involves governments purchasing any unsold surplus at the supported price if market price falls below the guaranteed level.

Recall one of the demand shifters: the number of buyers. Our original demand curve depicts the relationship between the price of sugar and quantity demanded by consumers, where the government is not included as a buyer. We can therefore model government purchases as an increase in the number of buyers in the sugar market: the demand curve for sugar shifts outward.

Demand shifts, but by how much? With this policy, the government does not purchase sugar indiscriminately; it buys only the amount needed to support the target price of €3 per kilo. The policy's objective pins down the exact shift in demand.

Consider a typical farmer in the EU. That farmer has no incentive to sell sugar below €3, the price at which the government promised to buy any unsold output. The same logic applies to all farmers. Therefore, the new equilibrium price must be €3. The market clears only when quantity supplied at €3 equals total quantity demanded, which now includes government purchases.

Our equilibrium condition from Module 3 becomes:

\(Q_S = Q_D + Q_{GOV}\)

where \(Q_{GOV}\) denotes the quantity purchased by government. Using the supply and demand functions from Modules 1-2, and substituting €3 for \(P\), we can find the quantity purchased by the government.

Quantity supplied at €3:
\(Q_S = 15P = 15(3) = 45\)
Consumer quantity demanded at €3:
\(Q_D = 50 - 10P = 50 - 10(3) = 20\)
Government purchases:
\(45 = 20 + Q_{GOV}\), so \(Q_{GOV} = 25\) million kg

We can visualize this as an outward shift in demand by 25 million kg. The new demand curve, which includes government as a purchaser, meets the supply curve at €3.

Interpreting the Demand Shift Result

The new equilibrium is \(P' = €3\)/kg and \(Q' = 45\) million kg/year — compared to \(P^* = €2\) and \(Q^* = 30\) million kg/year before the policy.

What to Notice

With the price support policy, consumers reduced their sugar purchases to 20 million kg. At the same time, producers increased their output to 45 million kg. These market responses are identical to those under the price floor set at €3 in Module 3. A major difference between the two policies is the fiscal cost that comes with the price support mechanism: the government purchases the entire surplus (the excess of quantity supplied over quantity demanded by consumers) at the supported price. Because all the surplus is purchased by the government, all producers are better off with this program.

Price Support, at What Cost?

Price support results in overproduction. Producers could have used their resources to produce alternative goods to meet other consumer demands. This represents a type of economic cost we will explore later in the course.

We now have all the information we need to calculate the fiscal cost of this program to the government. The government buys 25 million kg of sugar at €3/kg. Ignoring all other costs such as storage or disposal of sugar surplus, the government bears a fiscal cost of €75 million per year.

Figure 4.6: Fiscal cost of the price support equals the supported price times the government purchase quantity.

This example illustrates why the CAP created enormous costs. The surplus is not a one-time problem: as long as the price support remains above equilibrium, surpluses continue to accumulate year after year. Multiply €75 million by the number of years the policy ran, add storage costs across multiple commodities (sugar, butter, wine, grains, dairy), and the fiscal burden becomes staggering. The CAP consumed roughly 40% of the entire EU budget for decades.

This example also illustrates a broader point about policymaking. Policy interventions rarely make everyone better off. While CAP was intended to support farmers with stable prices, the costs were borne by consumers and taxpayers.

In the next section, we focus on the effect of CAP on foreign agricultural markets, which requires us to shift our attention to the supply side of the market.

Agricultural Dumping: A Supply Shifter in Other Countries

The price support programs under CAP created a ripple effect in foreign agricultural markets. While some surplus was left to rot in government warehouses, some was sold at low prices to other countries' markets.

Agricultural Dumping and Its Global Impact

CAP has historically provided considerable support for European sugar producers. Prior to the EU sugar market reform of 2017, which abolished production quotas, estimates suggested that approximately 5 million tons of excess EU sugar were dumped on world markets annually as a direct consequence of intervention buying incentivizing overproduction.

For markets in countries like Mozambique or Eswatini (formerly Swaziland), this arrival of cheap European sugar acts as a large positive supply shift: quantity supplied increases dramatically at every price. The result? Equilibrium price in those markets falls, and local sugar producers — who cannot compete with low European prices — are forced to cut output or exit the market entirely.

CAP thus illustrates how a policy designed to help domestic producers can simultaneously harm producers in developing countries while benefiting their consumers.

To illustrate this point with a numerical example, consider the Southern African sugar market. When we study a market in a particular country, we use the currency and units of measurement of that country. For consistency with previous sections, we retain the same currency and units (€ and kg).

Demand and supply in the Southern African sugar market are given by the following functions:

$$Q_D = 75 - 15P \qquad Q_S = 10P$$

The equilibrium is \(P^* = 3\) €/kg and \(Q^* = 30\) million kg/year. Suppose 15 million kg of the total surplus purchased by the EU are sold into the Southern African sugar market regardless of the local market price. This arrival of European sugar shifts supply outward.

Interpreting the Supply Shift Result

The new equilibrium is \(P' = €2.4\)/kg and \(Q' = 39\) million kg/year — compared to \(P^* = €3\) and \(Q^* = 30\) million kg/year before the EU entered.

Again, notice that the EU supplied 15 extra units, but total quantity only rose by 9 (from 30 to 39). The price drop (from 3 to 2.4) causes local Southern African producers to reduce their own output — they move down along their supply curve. This partial offset means the EU's actions crowd out some domestic supply.

CAP's buyback program thus supports sugar producers inside the EU while simultaneously lowering prices for competing producers in markets that receive the excess supply. Sugar consumers in Southern Africa benefit from lower prices and greater availability.

The CAP example demonstrates a crucial lesson: understanding demand and supply is essential for predicting the real-world effects of policy. A well-intentioned program designed to help farmers created surpluses, inefficient production, and massive costs to taxpayers, while also harming EU consumers and foreign farmers. By applying supply-and-demand analysis, we can anticipate these consequences before implementing a policy and design better alternatives.

While we focused on a particular policy as a driver of demand and supply shifts, such changes can happen due to other reasons. In the next section, you will practice the four-step process for equilibrium analysis with shifts driven by a variety of factors.

The Four-Step Process for Equilibrium Analysis

When an economic event affects demand or supply, use this systematic four-step process to predict the new equilibrium price and quantity. It works for any market and any type of change.

Summary

Module 4 expanded your toolkit for understanding markets. Price is not the only factor affecting markets; changes in income, tastes, technology, input costs, and expectations drive shifts in demand and/or supply. The critical distinction is between movements along curves (caused by price changes) and shifts of curves (caused by other factors).

You also saw how a price support policy can function as a demand shifter in the domestic EU market (raising price and quantity for EU producers) and as a supply shifter in foreign markets (lowering price and crowding out local producers).
